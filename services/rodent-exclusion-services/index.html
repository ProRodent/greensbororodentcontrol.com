Frequently asked

Common questions

What materials do you use for rodent exclusion sealing?

The right material depends on the gap type, location, and species. For vent openings and frame gaps, we use 1/4-inch hardware cloth (galvanized or stainless-steel mesh) in custom-fitted frames. For pipe penetrations, we use copper mesh packed into the gap followed by a suitable sealant, never foam alone. Rodents chew through within weeks. For foundation-block mortar gaps, we use lime-compatible patching mortar with copper mesh backing. For door sweeps and thresholds, we use heavy-gauge aluminum brush seals. Expanding foam is used only as a backing material behind rodent-grade mesh, never as the primary seal.

Does exclusion work if I still have active rodents in the building?

Exclusion must be sequenced correctly, trapping first, sealing second. If we seal a building with active rodents inside, we wall in the population. Trapped rodents die in walls, decompose, and produce odor. The correct protocol is always: trap and confirm the population is cleared, then seal every entry point. We never reverse this sequence. If you've already had exclusion work done by someone else and still have active rodents. That usually means either the sealing was incomplete or new entry points developed after the seal.

How long does rodent exclusion last?

Properly installed rodent-grade exclusion sealing is mostly permanent for frame gaps, hardware cloth in a secure frame at a foundation vent will hold for decades. The maintenance items are the elements that is flexible material or moving parts: door sweeps degrade over 3-5 years and should be inspected annually. Sealant at pipe penetrations can crack in freeze-thaw cycles and should be checked after hard winters. Vegetation that re-grows into roofline contact can create new pressure on attic seals. We says an perimeter check every 2-3 years for most Greensboro homes.

Can I do rodent exclusion myself?

Partially. Hardware cloth at obvious foundation vent openings, door sweep replacement. Copper mesh at visible pipe penetrations are DIY-open. The gaps that needs professional exclusion are the ones that needs knowing where to look. Sill-plate gaps at wood-to-concrete transitions, subtle gaps at utility-line entries, soffit-return separations that are only visible from the roofline. The secondary escape-route entry points that rodents use when primary entries are sealed. Incomplete DIY exclusion produces a slower infestation, not a solved one.
