Norway rat biology, why it matters for treatment

Our process

Both trapping and sealing come standard. One or two follow-up visits is standard. From start to finish, a week or two.

  • Neophobic: Norway rats are extremely cautious about new objects in their territory, including fresh traps. Successful snap-trap programs in crawl spaces needs pre-baiting, placing unset traps with bait for several days before the traps are activated. Skipping this step produces poor trap-strike rates and prolongs the program.
  • Burrow-based: The primary colony lives in ground burrows, not inside the frame. Crawl-space activity is an extension of the burrow network. Treating only the crawl space without addressing yard burrows leaves the source population intact.
  • Water-dependent: Norway rats need regular water access. Greensboro homes with standing water, leaking crawl-space pipes, condensate drain discharge onto the ground, or storm-drain adjacency sustain larger populations. Addressing moisture is part of the harborage-reduction assessment we provide.
  • Social and territorial: Norway rats live in social colonies of 10-100 individuals with a defined territory. Removing individuals without addressing the territory perimeter allows neighboring colony members to backfill within 1-2 weeks.

Frequently asked

Common questions

How do Norway rats get into Greensboro crawl spaces?

Norway rats enter crawl spaces through several common pathways: deteriorated vent screens (aluminum screen in time corrodes and holes develop), foundation block vents with mortar gaps that have widened over time. Utility-line penetrations through the foundation that were sealed with foam (foam alone isn't rodent-proof, they chew through it). Gaps at the sill plate where wood framing meets concrete foundation. In some cases through broken or misaligned sewer cleanout caps. The access points tend to be on the north and east faces of Greensboro foundations. Face away from prevailing wind and accumulate more moisture that accelerates deterioration.

What do Norway rat burrows look like in a Greensboro yard?

Norway rat burrows are 2-4 inch diameter holes in soil, usually with a small mound of fresh dirt at the opening and a secondary escape hole nearby with no dirt mound. They concentrate near harborage, under sheds, wood piles, decks, and near foundation walls. Along fences adjacent to restaurant alleys or storm-drain outfalls, you may see multiple burrows in a linear pattern following the fence line. Active burrows have clean, smooth-edged openings; inactive ones accumulate debris and spider webs.

Does bait or trapping work better for Norway rats in crawl spaces?

For crawl-space Norway rat programs, we usually use a combination. Snap traps are placed in the crawl space itself at confirmed travel paths. They provide fast knockdown of the existing population and produce carcasses we can remove rather than leaving them to decompose in the frame. Tamper-resistant bait stations are placed on the outside perimeter, targeting the broader colony that extends into yard burrows and storm-drain corridors. Bait alone in crawl spaces risks rodents dying inside the frame and producing odor.

Why do Norway rats keep coming back to my Greensboro property every year?

Annual recurrence has two causes that both need addressing. First, unsealed entry points: Norway rats from the storm-drain network, adjacent alleys. Neighboring homes can re-enter through any gap that wasn't sealed. Trapping the current population without sealing is a temporary fix. Second, harborage on the home: wood piles, dense shrubbery against the foundation, bird feeders, compost bins. Unsecured trash all sustain a resident Norway rat population between job cycles. We address both in our programs, exclusion sealing plus a written harborage-reduction assessment.
