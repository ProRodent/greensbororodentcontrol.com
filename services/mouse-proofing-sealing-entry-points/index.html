The kitchen inspection checklist, the most commonly missed mouse entries

  • →Under-sink drain pipe gap: Where the P-trap drain exits through the cabinet back panel or floor. Almost always present in Greensboro homes, 1/2 to 1 inch in diameter, directly adjacent to kitchen food storage.
  • →Gas supply line gap: Where the gas line enters the wall behind the range, usually foam-sealed originally, but foam degrades and is mouse-permeable. Needs copper mesh replacement.
  • →Cabinet toe-kick gaps: The gap between the cabinet base and the floor, mostly at corners where the toe-kick panel meets the adjacent wall. Mice use this concealed space as a primary travel corridor.
  • →Refrigerator water-line penetration: Where the ice-maker supply line exits through the wall behind the refrigerator. Gap is often 3/4 inch, in a rarely-inspected location.
  • →Dishwasher utility penetrations: Water supply, drain, and electrical conduit behind the dishwasher, multiple penetrations often at floor level, each a potential mouse entry.
  • →HVAC floor register gaps: Where floor-mounted HVAC registers sit over sub-floor duct penetrations, gaps around the register perimeter give mice wall-cavity access from below.

Frequently asked

Common questions

What gap size keeps mice out?

House mice can pass through a gap of about 6mm (1/4 inch), roughly the diameter of a pencil. This is a lot smaller than the rat threshold. Is why mouse-proofing needs more thorough sealing than rat-proofing. A gap that's too small for a rat can still be a viable mouse entry. In practice, anything that passes a 1/4-inch calibrated probe needs to be sealed. Standard hardware cloth with 1/4-inch mesh is mouse-grade; 1/2-inch mesh is not, mice can compress through half-inch openings.

What's the most commonly missed mouse entry point in Greensboro homes?

The gap around the drain pipe under the kitchen sink, mostly where the P-trap drain enters the cabinet back panel or floor. This gap is almost always present, often 1/2 to 1 inch in diameter. It's located directly adjacent to the kitchen food-storage area. The second most-missed point is the gap where the gas supply line enters the wall behind the range. A small gap that's often foam-sealed but rarely sealed with rodent-grade materials. Homeowners looking for their own entry points should check every pipe penetration in every cabinet before looking anywhere else.

How is mouse-proofing different from rat-proofing?

Two differences. First, the gap threshold: mouse-proofing needs sealing gaps down to 6mm rather than 20mm for rats. Means more entry points qualify and more thorough sealing is required. Second, the target locations: mice primarily exploit inside pipe penetrations, door sweeps. Wall-cavity entry points at kitchen and bathroom level. Rats primarily exploit foundation-level and roofline-level entries. Mouse-proofing focuses more heavily on the kitchen and utility-room anatomy of the home. Rat-proofing focuses on the building envelope at ground and roof level.

Does mouse-proofing require treating the scent trail too?

For recurring mouse problems, yes. Mouse-proofing seals the entry but doesn't erase the pheromone trail that guided mice to that entry. If the scent trail remains active at a newly sealed entry point, mice will probe the seal aggressively. If the seal has any weakness, the scent trail directs them to exploit it. For repeat-infestation homes, we combine physical sealing with enzymatic scent-trail treatment at confirmed travel routes.
