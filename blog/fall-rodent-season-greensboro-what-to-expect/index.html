Fall Rodent Season in Greensboro, What to Expect September Through December

By Greensboro Rodent Control

Fall is the peak rodent season in Greensboro. Cool nights drive rodents indoors.

Greensboro Specific November 17, 2026 5 min read

Mice come first. They fit through gaps the size of a dime.

Why September through December is peak season in Greensboro

Rats come next. They want shelter, food, and warmth.

Most fall calls start in October. Peak runs through December.

Month-by-month, how the fall season progresses

Signs: droppings, gnaw marks, sounds in walls, food packages chewed.

Best time to seal is late summer. Before the rush.

Once rodents are in, sealing alone does not fix it. Trap first.

Then seal entry points. Then follow up.

What you can do in August and September to stay ahead of the season

The most good fall preparation is a pre-season Check in August or early September, before the October influx peaks. A pre-season check spot foundation vent screens that have deteriorated over the summer, tree limbs that have grown into roofline contact, door sweeps that need replacement, and any existing evidence of early-season work that shows the population has already started arriving.

Check plus sealing in August or September converts the normal homeowner have, "I got surprised by rats in October again". Into a different have where the entry points that have produced the annual infestation are closed before the population arrives. The cost of a pre-season inspections and sealing is often less than the cost of a mid-season jobs for an established infestation. The five-day fall rodent-proofing checklist for Greensboro homes

If August is the time to Plan professional work, the weekend you can give it yourself is the time to do the DIY layer that complements it. Five days of focused attention each fall closes most of what professional sealing addresses. Without the cost, for households whose issues don't warrant full professional work.

Day 1, Edge inspection and gap stock. Walk the outside of the house slowly, flashlight in hand even in daylight (it picks up gaps the eye misses). Look at the foundation on all four sides for damaged vent screens, deteriorated mortar joints, settled gaps at the sill plate. Look at the roofline for soffit-return separations, missing trim, gable-vent screen damage. Look at every utility penetration, gas line, electric service, AC condensate, dryer vent, plumbing, for gaps around the sealant. Snap everything. Make a list with rough sizes and spots.

Day 2, Garage and outbuilding checks. Garages are the highest-volume rodent entry path most homeowners overlook. Check the garage door sweep along its entire length for compression gaps at the corners. Check the service-door threshold and side-frame seal. Check any wall penetrations from the garage to the main home, gas, water, electrical, for gaps. Repeat this checks for any detached outbuilding that abuts the home.

Day 3, Inside vulnerability check. Pull out the range and look behind it. Check the gas-line penetration through the wall. Open the cabinets under every kitchen and bathroom sink. Look at the drain and supply-line penetrations through the cabinet floor. Open the access panel for your HVAC return, check around the boot for cavity gaps. Snap what you find. Some of these will be unfixable without professional access. But knowing where the vulnerabilities are helps you response fast if you notice work later.

Day 4, Targeted sealing.Hardware store run: 1/4-inch hardware cloth (one roll covers most homes). Copper mesh (for stuffing into small gaps), outside-grade polyurethane sealant, garage door sweep if needed. Address every outside gap you snap on Day 1, starting with the largest. Material copper mesh into pipe penetrations before sealing them, the mesh prevents rodents chewing back through the sealant. Replace damaged vent screens with new hardware cloth in custom-cut frames. Six to eight hours of focused work on most homes.

Day 5, Indoor preparation. Even with perfect outside sealing, the occasional mouse will still get in through a gap nobody saw. Be ready: snap traps positioned in the garage, basement, and any utility room where work is most likely (in cabinets is fine for the storage areas mice access. Behind appliances if you have a known work pattern). Fresh bait (peanut butter works); reset and check weekly. The goal isn't to catch every mouse. It's to catch the first one within a few days so the issue never reaches "set up group."

Households with pets and children, what changes during fall rodent season

For families with dogs, cats. Young children, the fall rodent surge creates set about that warrant adjustments to standard advice. The risks aren't theoretical, the CDC documents pediatric exposure cases tied to mouse droppings in living spaces, and pet-poisoning calls involving bait goods spike every fall as homeowners deploy off-the-shelf bait.

For households with pets, the most important advice is what Not to do: don't use bait goods inside the home, even in cabinets or behind appliances where you think pets can't reach. Anticoagulant bait that a dog or cat accesses can be lethal. The secondary exposure (pet consuming a poisoned rodent) is also a real but smaller risk. Snap traps in inaccessible spots are the right choice, behind range, behind refrigerator, in cabinets with child locks, in utility rooms with doors closed. They produce recoverable carcasses (important for confirming what species you're dealing with) and there's no toxic residue concern.

For households with young children, focus on prevention of exposure to droppings rather than just trapping. Children explore low tops (floor, baseboards, base cabinets) where droppings often accumulate. The behaviors that increase Hantavirus and salmonella exposure risk are exactly the behaviors young children naturally does. Putting hands on spoiled tops, then in their mouths. Quick HEPA-vacuum cleanup of any visible droppings (with the child out of the room in work) and antimicrobial wipe-down of the area is the right response. Not letting it sit for "later."

What NC State Extension recommends before rodents move in

NC State Extension describes a complete rodent program as four parts: monitoring, sanitation, exclusion, and control. Monitoring means looking for droppings, chewed items, and nests. Sanitation means cleaning up food waste and moving compost bins, animal feed, and other attractants away from the house and other structures. Exclusion means sealing the places where rodents get in and out of buildings. Control means traps, poison, or both, and the guidance advises using exclusion devices to prevent accidental ingestion of rodent poison.

Extension also explains why small problems grow quickly. Mice can breed at 6 to 10 weeks old and have 5 to 10 litters a year, and rats breed at about 3 months and have 4 to 6 litters a year. In its words, just a few rats or mice can become many within a year or two. Doing the first three steps in late summer is far easier than catching up once a population is established.

Source: NC State Extension (Henderson County), House Mice and Other Rodents
