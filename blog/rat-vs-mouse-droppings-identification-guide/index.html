Fresh vs. old droppings: how to judge how recent the activity is

Fresh droppings are dark and moist with a slightly shiny tops. They harden and lighten in color as they age, often within 48-72 hours they begin losing moisture and turning gray or dusty. Older droppings crumble when touched. Very old droppings are dull, dry, and disintegrate easily.

Finding a mix of fresh dark droppings and older gray ones shows ongoing work, both historical and current. Finding only old, gray, crumbling droppings may show a past infestation that has resolved. Still, it can also mean work has moved to a different area of the frame. We use a fresh-dropping test in checks: wipe an area clean, return after 24-48 hours, and check for new deposits. New droppings confirm active current infestation. None found suggests either resolved work or a different zone.

What to do when you find droppings in a Greensboro home

Don't sweep or vacuum dry droppings with standard gear. Hantavirus and other rodent-borne pathogens become aerosolized when dry droppings are disturbed. Wet the droppings with a diluted bleach solution (1:10 ratio), let sit for 5 minutes. Then Wipe up with disposable paper towels and dispose in a sealed bag. Wear gloves. If the accumulation is large, attic-wide, crawl-space-wide, professional HEPA-vacuum cleanup is the right standard.

What dropping count tells you about colony size in your Greensboro home

A single rodent produces a predictable amount of droppings per 24-hour period. House mice excrete 50-80 fecal pellets daily. Norway rats produce 30-50. Roof rats produce 25-45. These rates are well-logged in pest-control biology literature and don't vary much with diet or work level.

The practical use: count the droppings in a defined area, divide by the daily-rate-per-animal, and you have a rough estimate of how many animals are using that area. The math isn't perfect, droppings persist for weeks before degrading, so a count includes accumulation across time. But the order of magnitude is reliable.

Example math. You find 200 mouse-sized droppings concentrated in a 2-foot section behind your kitchen range. If you last cleaned thoroughly behind the range four weeks ago, that's 200 droppings ÷ 28 days = 7 droppings per day in that spots. At 50-80 droppings per mouse per day, that's not even one mouse's full daily output landing in that specific spot. The actual group is more, those droppings show only the fraction landing in that one spots while the mice travel through. The total group is probably 2-5 animals using that travel path.

By contrast: 200 droppings in the same 2-foot zone if you cleaned Last week would show a lot heavier work, perhaps 5-10 mice making regular use of that path. The same evidence has very different group implications depending on the accumulation timeline.

This is part of why we ask Greensboro homeowners in check when they last cleaned the affected areas. The cleaning date converts visible evidence into a deposition rate, and the deposition rate is what reveals actual current population size.

Dropping color and texture, what it reveals about timeline and diet

Beyond size and shape, dropping appearance carries more info that helps narrow down what's happening and when:

Color tells you about diet. This can help confirm species. House mice with pantry access produce light-brown droppings (carbohydrate-rich diet). Norway rats with crawl-space access and outdoor foraging produce darker brown to black droppings (varied diet including meat scraps from outdoor scavenging). Roof rats with attic access primarily eating insulation, stored nuts, or fruit produce dark brown droppings often with visible flecks of nesting material. A homeowner finding light-brown droppings in a kitchen is almost sure dealing with mice, not rats, color confirms the size-based ID.

Texture and moisture tell you about timeline. Fresh droppings (under 48 hours) are dark, moist on the surface, and soft when carefully pressed with a tool. They have a slight sheen. Droppings 48 hours to 2 weeks old are darker, dry on the tops. But still firm internally. Droppings older than 2-3 weeks become crumbly, lighter brown, and break apart easily. A homeowner finding both fresh and old droppings in the same spots is seeing an active, ongoing infestation, not just historical work that ended.

Distribution pattern tells you about behavior. Droppings concentrated near food sources (cabinet undersides, pantry edges, behind ranges) show a feeding station. Droppings along travel routes (running parallel to baseboards, edge of garage walls) show transit paths. Droppings clustered in specific corners or behind insulation show a likely nesting spots nearby. The pattern across the home reveals the rodent's use of the space, where they eat, travel, and rest. This informs where to place traps for maximum effectiveness.

When dropping ID becomes hard, confusing cases and what to do

The size and shape rules work cleanly in textbook examples. In real Greensboro homes, several scenarios make ID harder than the rules suggest, and knowing the harder cases helps you response appropriately when you find one.

Mixed-group droppings. Homes with both mouse and rat work (more common than homeowners realize in older Greensboro neighborhoods) produce droppings of both sizes in overlapping spots. The reflex is to assume the bigger droppings are the "real" infestation and the smaller ones are secondary. That's not always correct. Two species can be active at once, with the smaller species sometimes representing the larger or longer-established population. Snap of a representative sample (including a ruler for scale) sent to an inspector are often more reliable than trying to interpret mixed evidence by eye.

Degraded or partial droppings. Droppings that have been disturbed, partially cleaned, or weathered (in garage or crawl-space environments) can lose the diagnostic shape characteristics. A 1/4-inch fragment of what was at first a 3/4-inch dropping looks like a fresh mouse pellet. Looking at the freshest, most-intact specimens for ID and treating partial fragments as supporting evidence rather than primary ID source produces better outcome.

Non-rodent droppings that look similar. Squirrel droppings (in attics and garages) are similar in size to roof rat droppings, both around 1/2 inch, both with somewhat pointed ends. Bat droppings (rare in Greensboro. But possible in older Irving Park or Aycock homes with attic access) are smaller, drier, and crumble to powder when pressed. If the spots of the droppings is steady with a non-rodent species. Mostly if you've seen squirrel work around the home, the ID may not be a rodent at all. Professional check resolves these uncertain cases.

Safe cleanup of droppings, according to the CDC

If you find droppings, resist the urge to sweep or vacuum them. The CDC says not to vacuum or sweep rodent urine, droppings, or nesting materials. Instead, put on rubber or plastic gloves and air out the space for 30 minutes. Spray the droppings and urine until very wet with a bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water, mixed fresh) or an EPA-registered disinfectant, and let it soak for 5 minutes.

Wipe it up with paper towels and throw them in a covered trash can. Mop or sponge hard surfaces with disinfectant, and wash your hands with soap and warm water afterward, including before you remove your gloves.

Source: CDC, How to Clean Up After Rodents
