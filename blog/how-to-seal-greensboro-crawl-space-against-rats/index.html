How to Seal a Greensboro Crawl Space Against Rats, What Works and What Doesn't

By Greensboro Rodent Control

Crawl space sealing keeps rats out for good. Here's how we do it.

Exclusion & Prevention November 17, 2026 8 min read

First, the vents. Old screens rust. Mesh tears. We replace all of them.

The gap threshold: what size gap lets a Norway rat through

Smaller than most people expect. UC IPM notes that young rats can squeeze under a door with a gap of only 1/2 inch, and it advises sealing any crack or opening larger than 1/4 inch. Mice need even less room: vent openings of 1/4 inch or smaller keep house mice out, according to the Wildlife Damage Management guide. As a rule of thumb, treat any gap you could slide a pencil into as an entry point.

Steel hardware cloth with 1/4-inch mesh, screwed down tight, is the standard material for crawl-space vents. The rim joist, where the floor meets the foundation wall, is the next place to check.

What doesn't work, the materials that fail within a season

Plumbing entries are the third spot. Pipes pass through the rim.

Old caulk fails. New foam fills the gap. Then caulk for the finish.

Foundation cracks need patch. Mortar or hydraulic cement works.

What does work, material specs for each crawl-space entry type

Last, the crawl door. Often poorly sealed. We add a sweep.

Sill-plate gaps: The junction between the wood sill plate and the concrete or block foundation. These gaps widen over time as wood moves, they may be hairline in some places and 1/2 inch in others on the same home. Copper mesh packed into open gaps, followed by foam backer rod and outside-grade sealant. For larger gaps, rigid materials backing (galvanized flashing) before the mesh and sealant.

Crawl-space access door: Heavy-gauge aluminum brush sweep at the door threshold, not the hollow rubber bulb sweeps that compress under light pressure. Hardware-cloth frame at any ventilated panel in the access door itself.

The sequencing rule, why you seal last, not first

The most consequential error in DIY crawl-space sealing is sealing before the existing group is cleared. If Norway rats are now in the crawl space when you seal the entry points, you've sealed them in. Sealed rodents that can't exit die in the crawl space, decompose, and produce weeks of odor that migrates into the living space above. The resulting cleanup is more costly and more disruptive than addressing the population before sealing.

The correct sequence: confirm whether there's an active population first. Signs of active crawl-space infestation include fresh droppings (dark and moist, not gray and crumbling), musky odor from the access hatch, and scratching sounds below floor level. If those signs are present, trap the population before sealing, snap traps in the crawl space at confirmed travel paths. Pre-baited for 3-5 days before start to overcome Norway rat neophobia. Once trap work has stopped for 5 or more days, the population is likely cleared and sealing can go.

When professional exclusion is worth the cost versus DIY

Foundation vent screen replacement with hardware cloth is a straightforward DIY task for an open, outside-mounted vent with a simple frame. Pipe penetrations at open outside walls are manageable with copper mesh and suitable sealant. These are the two DIY-open interventions that cover the most common entry points.

The entry points that wins from professional checks are the ones that need knowing where to look: sill-plate gaps at inside corners of the crawl space that are only visible from inside, secondary pipe penetrations at utility chases that don't show on the outside, and the subtle separations at the wood-frame junction where post-war additions meet the first foundation. These gaps are the ones that keep producing recurring infestations after a homeowner has addressed the obvious vents and thinks the job is done.

Common DIY mistakes that create new problems

Roughly 20% of our crawl-space sealing calls in Greensboro come from homeowners who tried DIY sealing first and either failed or created new issues. The five patterns that come up most often:

Foam-only sealing of pipe penetrations. Expanding spray foam alone doesn't stop rats, they chew through it within days. The correct method is to material copper mesh wool into the gap first. Then Seal with foam or sealant on top. The mesh gives the actual barrier. The foam holds it in place and finishes the seal. DIY work that uses foam alone has often failed within 30 days.

Stapling hardware cloth to old sill plate. Staples driven into aged sill-plate wood (especially the soft, moisture-degraded wood common in older Greensboro homes) pull out within one or two seasons. The mesh patch detaches; the gap reopens. The correct method is galvanized screws driven through the mesh and into solid framing. Often the rim joist or band joist behind the sill plate, every 6 inches along the mesh edge.

Sealing the wrong gap and missing the real one.Homeowners often address the visually obvious gap (the missing vent screen they can see from outside) and miss the less-obvious entry path that's really being used (the pipe penetration on the opposite side. The soffit-return gap that's harder to see). Norway rats follow fixed travel routes. Sealing a non-route entry point achieves nothing if the actual route remains open. A planned walk-around looking for grease trails, droppings, and gnaw marks spot which gaps the rats are really using.

Sealing in active infestation. Already talked at length in the sequencing rule section above, sealing entry points before confirming group clearance traps rodents inside. The most common DIY mistake we see, and the source of the worst follow-up problems (dead rodents in walls, secondary odor issues). Much higher total cost than if the work had been done in correct sequence).

Using galvanized hardware cloth on a heritage home. For homeowners in Irving Park, Fisher Park, Aycock, and other older Greensboro neighborhoods, standard galvanized hardware cloth installed with visible screws on architecturally large parts (first soffits. Decorative wood vent frames, period brickwork) can for good damage character-defining features. Heritage-aware install uses stainless mesh in custom-fitted frames and no-drill attachment ways. The material cost is 2-3x higher. The other is irreversible damage to features the home's resale value depends on.

What university extension guides say about sealing materials

UC IPM's rat guide says rats can squeeze under a door with a gap as small as 1/2 inch and advises sealing cracks and openings larger than 1/4 inch with wire screen or sheet metal. It also warns against lightweight chicken wire, because rats can pass through the openings.

The Wildlife Damage Management guide to rodent-proof construction specifies 1/4-inch galvanized hardware cloth for vents and 24-gauge or heavier galvanized sheet metal for collars and flashing, held in place with sheet metal screws or urethane foam caulk. Both sources point the same way: use hard materials that a rodent cannot chew or squeeze through, and do not rely on foam or soft mesh alone.

Source: UC IPM, Rats; Wildlife Damage Management, Rodent-Proof Construction
